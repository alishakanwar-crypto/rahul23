## PART III: LEGAL ASSESSMENT — WHY THE APPLICATION MUST FAIL

### 1. Para 603 is Dispositive Under O.VII R.11

Under O.VII R.11, the Court must look at the **averments in the plaint ALONE** — not at extraneous material, not at the Defendant's interpretation, not at what the Defendant wishes the plaint said.

**Para 603 of the Plaint EXPRESSLY pleads:** The suit is by a co-owner in joint and constructive possession, seeking partition. Not a suit by an excluded stranger.

The Application cannot override this by selectively quoting other paragraphs. The plaint must be read AS A WHOLE — and as a whole, it pleads constructive joint possession.

**Case Law:**
- **Dahiben v. Arvindbhai Kalyanji Bhanusali, (2020) 7 SCC 366:** For O.VII R.11, the averments in the plaint must be taken at face value; the court cannot dissect plaint allegations to arrive at a conclusion contrary to what the plaint itself states.
- **Sopan Sukhdeo Sable v. Assistant Charity Commissioner, (2004) 3 SCC 137:** The plaint has to be read as a whole to ascertain its true nature; the court cannot cull out individual sentences to determine the suit's character.

### 2. Partition Suit by Co-Owner = Fixed Court Fee — Settled Law

In a partition suit where the Plaintiff claims as co-owner and seeks partition/separate possession, only FIXED court fee is payable under Article 17(vi) of Schedule II, Court Fees Act, 1870.

**The fact that the Plaintiff seeks delivery of possession does NOT convert the suit into a "suit for possession" requiring ad valorem fee.** Every partition suit seeks separate possession — that is the very nature of partition.

**Case Law:**
- **Satheedevi v. Prasanna Kumar, (2010) 5 SCC 601:** In a suit for partition, the plaintiff who claims as a co-owner is required to pay only a fixed court fee. The mere fact that the plaintiff also seeks delivery of separate possession does not make it a suit for "possession" requiring ad valorem court fee.
- **Meenakshisundaram v. Venkatachalam, (1979) 3 SCC 481:** Where the plaintiff sues for partition as a co-owner, court fee is payable under Schedule II, Article 17(vi) regardless of whether separate possession is also sought.

### 3. "Ouster from Family House" ≠ "Loss of Co-Ownership Status"

The Application's fundamental fallacy is treating the Plaintiff's departure from the family residence (after physical assault) as equivalent to losing his status as co-owner of the PPJ Corpus.

**In law:**
- A co-owner's title and constructive possession subsists UNTIL there is a formal partition or a decree declaring otherwise
- Physical exclusion from one property does not extinguish rights in the entire corpus
- **Suraj Bhan v. Financial Commissioner, (2007) 6 SCC 186:** A co-sharer's rights in joint family property are not extinguished by exclusion from physical possession; constructive possession continues
- **Dorab Cawasji Warden v. Coomi Sorab Warden, (1990) 2 SCC 117:** Even where a co-owner is out of physical possession of a specific property, his undivided interest and constructive possession in the joint estate subsists

### 4. Even if Court Fee Issue Exists — Proper Remedy is S.149, Not Rejection

Even assuming (without admitting) that there is some court fee deficiency:
- **Section 149 CPC** permits the court to allow deficient court fee to be made good
- The Plaintiff has ALREADY filed a Section 149 application (IA 16085/2026) which was allowed on 19.06.2026
- The proper course is to determine the correct court fee and direct the Plaintiff to pay the difference — NOT to reject the plaint

**Case Law:**
- **Noor Mohammad v. Jethanand, (2013) 5 SCC 202:** Where court fee is found insufficient, the court should ordinarily permit the plaintiff to make good the deficiency rather than rejecting the plaint under O.VII R.11
- **Shah Babulal Khimji v. Jayaben D. Kania, (1981) 4 SCC 8:** The power under O.VII R.11 is a drastic one and should be exercised sparingly; where the suit discloses a cause of action, the plaint should not be rejected merely on the ground of undervaluation

### 5. O.VII R.11 is a Drastic/Exceptional Power

Plaint rejection under O.VII R.11 is NOT routine — it is a drastic step that forecloses the Plaintiff's access to justice. The court should lean toward hearing the suit on merits rather than rejecting at threshold.

**Case Law:**
- **T. Arivandandam v. T.V. Satyapal, (1977) 4 SCC 467:** The power under O.VII R.11 should be exercised with circumspection and caution; the court should not lightly shut out a plaintiff from being heard
- **Hardesh Ores (P) Ltd. v. Hede & Co., (2007) 5 SCC 614:** Rejection of plaint is a drastic remedy and the court must be slow to exercise it

### 6. The Application is a Dilatory Tactic

The timing of the application (filed 20.06.2026, one day after notice issued on 19.06.2026) and the nature of the ground (court fee technicality) strongly suggest that the purpose is to:
- Delay hearing on IAs 16084 (status quo), 16087 (auditor), 16088 (disclosure), 16089 (maintenance)
- Prevent ad interim relief from being considered on 22.06.2026
- Buy time while assets continue to be alienated (Bahadurgarh plot already sold)

**Assessment:**
- A perjury application is technically maintainable because:
  - The affidavit states "nothing material has been concealed" — this is demonstrably false
  - Para 603 is the MOST material paragraph for a court fee challenge and its suppression is deliberate
  
- **HOWEVER, the more effective remedy is:**
  - File a detailed reply exposing the suppression
  - Seek exemplary costs for filing a misleading and frivolous application
  - The court is more likely to impose costs than initiate criminal prosecution for this type of suppression

### Recommended Approach: Costs + Contempt (not standalone perjury)

Rather than a standalone perjury application (which courts are reluctant to entertain), the reply should:
1. Expose the deliberate suppression of Para 603
2. Seek exemplary costs under Section 35-A CPC
3. Seek action under Delhi High Court (Original Side) Rules for misleading the court
4. Reserve right to file perjury application if the pattern continues

## PART V: BEST STRATEGY FOR INTERIM RELIEF

### The O7R11 Application Should NOT Delay Interim Relief

**Key Arguments:**

1. **O.VII R.11 and interim relief are independent:**
   - Even if the O7R11 application is pending, the court can grant interim relief
   - The jurisdiction to grant interim protection under O.39 R.1&2 is independent of the O.VII R.11 determination
   - **Wander Ltd. v. Antox India P. Ltd., 1990 Supp SCC 727:** Interim relief can be granted even when the maintainability of the suit is under challenge

2. **The very purpose of interim relief is to preserve the subject matter:**
   - If the court delays interim relief pending O7R11 determination, the PPJ Corpus may be further dissipated
   - Bahadurgarh property has already been sold — this demonstrates the urgency
   - **Deoraj v. State of Maharashtra, (2004) 4 SCC 697:** Where there is imminent danger of the subject matter being destroyed or dissipated, interim protection must be granted immediately

3. **Even if plaint is rejected under O7R11, fresh suit can be filed:**
   - O7R11 rejection is not on merits — Plaintiff can re-file after paying correct court fee
   - But dissipated assets CANNOT be recovered
   - Therefore, interim protection is more urgent than the court fee debate

4. **Plaintiff's offer under Section 149:**
   - Plaintiff has already offered to pay any additional court fee as directed
   - The Section 149 application was allowed on 19.06.2026
   - This demonstrates bona fides and removes any prejudice to Defendant

## PART VII: WEAKNESSES TO PREPARE FOR (Devil's Advocate)

The Application is NOT entirely without merit — be prepared for these arguments:

1. **The plaint DOES contain strong ouster language:**
   - "manhandled and physically assaulted" (Para 500)
   - "constrained to leave the family residence" (Para 429)
   - "has since then been residing in leased premises" (Para 429)
   - "excluded from all businesses, properties and income-generating assets for more than a decade" (Para 924)
   
   **Counter:** These describe exclusion from business MANAGEMENT and family RESIDENCE — not loss of co-ownership. A co-owner doesn't lose constructive possession merely by being physically excluded.

2. **Prayer C explicitly seeks possession of properties worth Rs. 81 Cr:**
   - This looks like a "suit for possession" on its face
   
   **Counter:** Every partition suit seeks separate possession — that's the nature of partition. Prayer A (the dominant relief) is for partition/preliminary decree. Prayer C is consequential to partition, not independent.

3. **Internal tension in the plaint between ouster narrative and constructive possession claim:**
   
   **Counter:** There is no contradiction. The Plaintiff was ousted from the family HOME and from MANAGEMENT of businesses. But as a co-owner whose 23% share has been admitted, he retains constructive possession in law. These are two legally distinct concepts. The plaint addresses both.
